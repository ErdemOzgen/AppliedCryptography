## Part 1: Why go post quantum

## The core problem

Modern public key cryptography depends on mathematical problems that are hard for classical computers but not necessarily hard for quantum computers.

The important distinction is this:

Classical security means:

`No known efficient classical algorithm can solve the underlying problem.`

Post quantum security means:

`No known efficient classical or quantum algorithm can solve the underlying problem.`

The deck starts with this exact motivation: quantum computers use superposition and entanglement, and those properties allow specific algorithms to solve some cryptographic problems much faster than classical machines. This matters because RSA, DH, and ECC are built on factoring or discrete logarithms, both of which are vulnerable to Shor’s algorithm. 

## Quantum computers do not simply try all keys in parallel

A common beginner mistake is saying:

`A quantum computer checks every possible key at once.`

That is not accurate.

A quantum computer can place amplitudes over many states, but measurement gives only one outcome. The useful computation comes from **interference**. Good paths are amplified, bad paths are canceled. This is why quantum algorithm design is difficult. You need a structure that lets amplitudes interfere in a useful way.

For cryptography, the two algorithms you must know are:

1. **Shor’s algorithm**

This attacks factoring and discrete logarithms. It is catastrophic for RSA, DH, ECDH, ElGamal, Schnorr, DSA, and ECDSA.

2. **Grover’s algorithm**

This gives a quadratic speedup for brute force search. It weakens symmetric crypto but usually does not destroy it.

## Practical mental model

Think of cryptographic primitives in three groups:

| Primitive type                         | Classical examples                | Quantum impact     |
| -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------ |
| Public key encryption and key exchange | RSA, DH, ECDH                     | Broken by Shor     |
| Digital signatures                     | RSA signatures, DSA, ECDSA, EdDSA | Broken by Shor     |
| Symmetric crypto and hashes            | AES, SHA 2, SHA 3, ChaCha20       | Weakened by Grover |

That table is the main threat model for the entire presentation.

## Why RSA, DH, and ECC fail

RSA relies on this assumption:

`Given N = p × q, finding p and q is hard.`

That is the factoring problem.

DH and ECC rely on this assumption:

`Given g and g^x, finding x is hard.`

That is the discrete logarithm problem.

For elliptic curve crypto, the group is not integers modulo p, but the structure is still a group, and the private key is still hidden inside a scalar multiplication relation.

Shor’s algorithm gives polynomial time quantum algorithms for factoring and discrete logarithms. That means the security assumption collapses, not just weakens. The deck explicitly states that public key crypto based on factoring or discrete logarithms can be broken by quantum computers. 

## Senior engineering takeaway

Do not say:

`RSA 4096 is quantum safe because it is bigger.`

It is not. Increasing RSA key size does not fix the fundamental issue. Shor’s algorithm scales polynomially. Bigger RSA may delay a quantum attacker somewhat, but it does not restore the exponential security assumption.

For quantum migration, RSA and ECC must be replaced or combined with post quantum algorithms.

## Grover’s algorithm and symmetric crypto

Grover’s algorithm attacks unstructured search.

For symmetric encryption, key recovery can be framed as search:

Given plaintext and ciphertext pairs:

`C = Enc(K, M)`

Find the key K such that decrypting C gives M.

Classically, a key of length λ costs about:

`2^λ`

With Grover, the cost becomes approximately:

`2^(λ ÷ 2)`

So AES 128 gives roughly 64 bit quantum search resistance under this simplified model, while AES 256 gives roughly 128 bit quantum search resistance.

That is why the deck recommends moving from AES 128 to AES 256 when long term quantum resistance is needed. It also notes that this advice is slightly simplified, especially for hash functions where the right output length depends on whether you care about preimage resistance, second preimage resistance, or collision resistance. 

## Important nuance about Grover

Grover is not magic parallel brute force.

It is fundamentally sequential in a way that makes naive parallelization inefficient. This matters operationally because you cannot simply assume that a nation state with one million quantum machines gets a perfect one million times speedup over Grover. The algorithm has structure, and query depth matters.

Practical advice:

Use AES 256 for long lived data.

Use SHA 384 or SHA 512 when conservative hash output size matters.

Do not panic about AES 128 for short lived sessions, but avoid choosing it for new high assurance long term designs.

## Shor versus Grover

The difference is severity.

Shor causes a **structural break**.

Grover causes a **security level reduction**.

In security review terms:

RSA, DH, ECC:

`Replace them.`

AES 128:

`Increase margin if needed.`

AES 256:

`Still conservative.`

SHA 256:

`Usually still useful, but check security goal.`

Signatures based on ECC:

`Replace them before quantum capable adversaries arrive.`

## Engineering reality of quantum computers

The deck is careful not to overhype current quantum computers. Current devices have small numbers of noisy qubits. They suffer from decoherence, gate errors, physical isolation issues, cooling requirements, and the massive overhead of quantum error correction. 

The most important concept here is **logical qubits versus physical qubits**.

A physical qubit is the actual noisy hardware element.

A logical qubit is an error corrected abstraction built from many physical qubits.

To run cryptographically meaningful attacks, you need many logical qubits and many reliable operations. Because current qubits are noisy, each logical qubit may require hundreds or thousands of physical qubits depending on architecture and error rates.

## Why coherence matters

Qubits must remain in a controlled quantum state long enough to complete computation.

Noise sources include:

| Noise source          | Why it matters                  |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Heat                  | Destroys fragile quantum states |
| Vibration             | Disturbs physical systems       |
| Electromagnetic noise | Corrupts qubit state            |
| Cosmic rays           | Can introduce faults            |
| Measurement           | Collapses the quantum state     |

For cryptography, this matters because breaking RSA or ECC is not a tiny toy computation. It requires sustained, fault tolerant computation.

## Current factorization records are misleading

The deck gives a strong reality check: many public claims about quantum factorization use tiny numbers or specially constructed cases. Factoring 15 or 21 is not evidence that RSA 2048 is near collapse. Some demonstrations use compiled circuits where the circuit is tailored with knowledge of the answer. 

Senior takeaway:

Do not evaluate quantum risk by toy factorization demos.

Evaluate it by:

1. Logical qubit requirements

2. Gate depth

3. Error correction cycle time

4. Physical qubit overhead

5. Attack target lifetime

6. Whether the target is online, offline, stored, or interactive

## The hype versus reality balance

The deck makes two points that seem contradictory but are both true.

First:

Current quantum computers are nowhere near breaking real RSA or ECC.

Second:

You still need to migrate now.

Why both are true:

Crypto migration takes years. Large systems have protocols, hardware, certificates, embedded devices, HSMs, smart cards, compliance requirements, legacy clients, and backward compatibility constraints.

Waiting until a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists is too late.

## Store now, decrypt later

This is one of the most important operational concepts in the deck.

An adversary can record encrypted traffic today, store it, and decrypt it later when quantum capabilities become available. This is especially relevant when current sessions use classical ECDH or RSA key transport.

Affected data includes:

| Data type                     | Risk                                        |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Medical records               | Confidentiality needed for decades          |
| Government traffic            | Long intelligence value                     |
| Source code and trade secrets | Long commercial value                       |
| Diplomatic communication      | Long strategic value                        |
| Personal messages             | Privacy lifetime may exceed crypto lifetime |

The deck explicitly highlights this as a present day attack because collection can happen before the decryption capability exists. 

## Practical risk formula

Use this formula:

`Risk = data lifetime + migration time greater than time to quantum capability`

This is often called Mosca’s inequality.

If data must remain secret for 20 years, and migration takes 7 years, then you are exposed if a quantum attacker appears within 27 years.

That is why some sectors must act earlier than others.

## Who should care first

The deck lists finance, healthcare, government, technology companies, critical infrastructure, and everyone else. 

From an engineering prioritization perspective:

| Priority | System type                                           | Why                              |
| -------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Highest  | Long term confidential data in transit                | Store now, decrypt later         |
| Highest  | Firmware signing roots                                | Hard to replace after deployment |
| High     | PKI roots and intermediates                           | Ecosystem migration is slow      |
| High     | VPN and TLS at scale                                  | Large exposure surface           |
| Medium   | Internal service mesh                                 | Easier to upgrade if controlled  |
| Medium   | User authentication tokens                            | Depends on token lifetime        |
| Lower    | Short lived symmetric session encryption with AES 256 | Already relatively safe          |

## The most dangerous area is signatures in hardware

Encryption failures leak past data.

Signature failures can destroy trust infrastructure.

If a quantum attacker can break an old signing key, they may be able to forge firmware, fake software updates, impersonate hardware, or bypass secure boot. This becomes especially dangerous in embedded devices where keys are burned into ROM or firmware update trust anchors cannot be replaced easily.

This is why post quantum migration is not only about TLS.

## What post quantum cryptography means

Post quantum cryptography means classical algorithms that run on normal computers but are believed to resist both classical and quantum attacks.

This is not quantum cryptography.

It does not require quantum networks.

It does not require quantum hardware.

It is software and protocol cryptography designed around different hard problems.

The deck lists these families:

| Family                | Main idea                                             |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Lattice based         | Hardness of noisy linear algebra and lattice problems |
| Code based            | Hardness of decoding random linear codes              |
| Hash based signatures | Security from hash preimage and collision resistance  |
| Multivariate          | Solving systems of nonlinear polynomial equations     |
| Isogeny based         | Maps between elliptic curves                          |

The currently dominant general purpose family is lattice based crypto.

## NIST standardization

Early selected algorithms included CRYSTALS Kyber for KEM, CRYSTALS Dilithium for signatures, Falcon for signatures, and SPHINCS plus for hash based signatures. 

Later in the deck, the standardized names are shown:

| Original name      | Standard name | Purpose            | Hardness basis |
| ------------------ | ------------- | ------------------ | -------------- |
| CRYSTALS Kyber     | ML KEM        | Key encapsulation  | Module LWE     |
| CRYSTALS Dilithium | ML DSA        | Digital signatures | Module LWE     |

| SPHINCS plus       | SLH DSA       | Digital signatures | Hash functions |

This naming matters because current engineering docs, RFCs, and libraries increasingly use the standard names rather than the competition names. 

## Key exchange versus KEM

Traditional DH style key exchange works like this:

Alice contributes randomness.

Bob contributes randomness.

Both derive a shared secret.

KEM works differently:

Bob has a public key and private key.

Alice generates or derives a shared secret.

Alice encapsulates it to Bob’s public key.

Bob decapsulates it using his private key.

The deck emphasizes that KEMs are the natural abstraction for many post quantum systems because lattice and code based assumptions do not naturally provide the same commutative structure as DH. 

## Practical protocol diagram

```text
Bob creates key pair

public key  pk
secret key  sk

Alice obtains pk

Alice runs Encaps(pk)

output:
ciphertext c
shared secret k

Alice sends c to Bob

Bob runs Decaps(sk, c)

output:
same shared secret k
```

Important point:

The ciphertext is not the shared secret. The ciphertext is public transport material. The shared secret must go through a KDF before being used for encryption.

## Hybrid crypto

Hybrid means combining classical and post quantum secrets.

Example:

`shared_secret = KDF(classical_secret ∥ post_quantum_secret ∥ transcript)`

Security goal:

The connection remains secure if at least one component remains secure.

This is critical because post quantum algorithms are newer and less battle tested than X25519 or P 256. Hybrid protects against both quantum risk and unexpected post quantum cryptanalysis.

The deck later applies this to X Wing, PQ3, Signal, and OpenSSH. 

## Migration principle

Do not replace stable classical crypto with immature post quantum crypto in a brittle way.

Prefer hybrid during the transition.

A good migration plan:

1. Inventory cryptographic assets

2. Classify data by confidentiality lifetime

3. Identify public key encryption and key agreement first

4. Add hybrid KEMs in TLS, SSH, VPN, messaging

5. Plan signature migration for PKI, firmware, and code signing

6. Track library maturity and side channel resistance

7. Avoid custom parameter choices

8. Design for algorithm agility

## Senior cryptography tips

### Tip 1: Do not confuse algorithm security with implementation security

A mathematically sound KEM can still leak its secret key through timing, cache behavior, power, or branch dependent execution.

The deck later gives KyberSlash as an example where timing leakage affected Kyber implementations. 

### Tip 2: Never design your own post quantum parameters

Post quantum security depends heavily on parameter selection. Small changes in dimension, modulus, noise distribution, compression, or rejection bounds can destroy security or correctness.

Use standardized parameter sets.

### Tip 3: Treat decapsulation as dangerous

KEM decapsulation processes attacker controlled ciphertext using a private key. That makes it a high value attack surface.

Implementation requirements:

Constant time behavior

No secret dependent branches

No secret dependent memory access

Uniform error handling

No detailed failure oracles

Careful fault attack protection for hardware

### Tip 4: Bind everything into the transcript

When combining KEMs with TLS, SSH, or messaging protocols, bind public keys, ciphertexts, algorithm identifiers, and context into the KDF transcript.

Otherwise downgrade and substitution attacks become possible.

### Tip 5: Hybrid composition must be done with a KDF, not XOR alone

Correct:

`KDF(secret1 ∥ secret2 ∥ context)`

Risky:

`secret1 xor secret2`

A KDF gives domain separation, extraction, and context binding.

### Tip 6: Post quantum does not automatically mean forward secrecy

A KEM can be static or ephemeral.

For forward secrecy, you need fresh ephemeral key material and deletion of old secrets.

For post compromise security, you need ratcheting, not just a one time post quantum handshake.

That is why protocols like PQ3 and Triple Ratchet matter later in the deck.

# 2.1 Learning with Errors and lattice crypto

## Core idea

Learning with Errors, usually called **LWE**, is the mathematical heart of many modern post quantum systems. The slide first reminds you that normal linear equations are easy. If you have:

`b = A s`

and you know `A` and `b`, then you can solve for `s` efficiently with linear algebra. Gaussian elimination, row reduction, matrix inversion, and related techniques all work because the equations are exact. The deck spends several slides showing row operations, pivots, row echelon form, and back substitution to build this intuition. 

LWE changes the equation to:

`b = A s + e`

where `e` is small noise.

That tiny noise destroys the exact algebraic structure that Gaussian elimination needs. You can still write the equations down, but every cancellation also transforms and mixes the noise. After a few row operations, the noise becomes polluted and hard to separate from the signal.

## Why this is cryptographically useful

The attacker sees:

`A`

`b = A s + e`

The attacker wants `s`.

Without the error term, this is easy. With the error term, it becomes hard. The deck states the key dichotomy: with many equations, there is usually at most one solution, but finding that solution appears extremely hard, including against known quantum algorithms. 

The LWE problem has two major forms:

1. Search LWE

Given many samples, recover the secret `s`.

2. Decision LWE

Given samples, distinguish whether they are real LWE samples or uniformly random values.

Modern cryptographic proofs often reduce security to the decision version. If an adversary cannot distinguish structured noisy samples from random samples, the ciphertexts look like noise.

## What “small” means modulo q

In LWE we work modulo `q`.

If `q = 11`, then `10` is not “large” in the centered sense. It is equivalent to `minus 1 mod 11`, so it is close to zero.

This matters because the error distribution is centered around zero. A value like `q minus 1` may actually represent a small negative error.

Senior implementation point:

Always be explicit about representation.

Values may be stored as unsigned integers from `0` to `q minus 1`, but mathematically interpreted as centered values near zero.

Many implementation bugs and side channels appear around reduction, compression, decompression, and centered conversion.

## LWE based KEM intuition

A KEM has three operations:

```
KeyGen gives public key pk and secret key sk

Encaps takes pk
Encaps outputs ciphertext c and shared secret K

Decaps takes sk and c
Decaps outputs the same shared secret K
```

The basic LWE KEM idea is:

1. Bob publishes noisy linear equations based on his secret.

2. Alice uses Bob’s public key to create another noisy value.

3. Bob uses his secret to cancel the large structured part.

4. What remains is the message plus small noise.

The deck shows this with a simplified scheme where Bob publishes `b = A s + e1`, Alice computes a ciphertext using random `r`, and Bob computes a value close to either zero or `q divided by 2` to recover a bit. The core cancellation is that the `r transpose A s` term appears on both sides and cancels, leaving only small errors plus the encoded bit. 

## Why decapsulation works

The trick is spacing.

If bit `0` is encoded near `0`, and bit `1` is encoded near `q divided by 2`, then small noise will not confuse them.

Imagine a circle modulo `q`.

Value near `0` means bit `0`.

Value near halfway around the circle means bit `1`.

As long as the accumulated error is much smaller than the gap between these regions, Bob can decode correctly.

Senior design point:

Correctness matters as much as secrecy. If your noise is too large, decryption fails. If your noise is too small, the scheme may leak structure and lose security. Parameter selection balances secrecy, correctness, bandwidth, and performance.

## Reconciliation

Because the computed values are noisy, both sides need a reliable way to derive exactly the same bits.

Reconciliation means adding structured helper information so the receiver can correct small disagreements without leaking the secret.

In production KEMs, this is not a casual add on. It is part of the security and correctness design. Incorrect reconciliation can create failure oracles.

## From LWE to Kyber and ML KEM

Plain LWE uses large random matrices, which are expensive to store and multiply.

Kyber, standardized as **ML KEM**, uses module lattice structure. Instead of arbitrary huge matrices, it uses polynomial ring arithmetic and module structure. This gives compact keys and fast operations.

The deck explains that Kyber improves the basic scheme by using structured matrices, Module LWE, careful parameters, a Fujisaki Okamoto style transform for chosen ciphertext security, and performance competitive with elliptic curve systems. 

## Fujisaki Okamoto transform

A basic public key encryption scheme may be secure only against passive attackers.

Real networks need security against active attackers who can submit malicious ciphertexts and observe behavior.

The Fujisaki Okamoto transform strengthens a scheme by making encryption randomness derived from the message and verifying ciphertext consistency during decryption. If the ciphertext is invalid, decapsulation must not leak useful information.

Senior engineering rule:

KEM decapsulation is dangerous because it processes attacker controlled ciphertext with a secret key.

You need:

1. Constant time decapsulation

2. Constant time failure handling

3. No detailed error messages

4. No early returns based on secret dependent checks

5. No timing difference between valid and invalid ciphertexts

6. Key confirmation only through authenticated protocol transcript logic

## ML KEM practical parameters

The deck lists three ML KEM levels:

1. ML KEM 512 targets AES 128 equivalent security

2. ML KEM 768 targets AES 192 equivalent security

3. ML KEM 1024 targets AES 256 equivalent security

It also compares ML KEM to X25519: ML KEM has a large bandwidth penalty, roughly 25 to 50 times larger public material, but can be computationally fast. The slide gives ML KEM 768 as 1184 client bytes and 1088 server bytes, while X25519 is 32 bytes on both sides. 

## Tips and tricks for LWE and ML KEM

1. Do not tune parameters yourself.

2. Use standardized ML KEM parameter sets.

3. Prefer ML KEM 768 for general purpose hybrid transport unless a standard profile says otherwise.

4. Treat decapsulation as an exposed attack surface.

5. Test invalid ciphertext behavior aggressively.

6. Use constant time libraries with real side channel review.

7. Bind algorithm identifiers, public keys, ciphertexts, and transcript context into the KDF.

8. Do not use raw KEM output directly as an AEAD key. Always pass it through a KDF.

# 2.2 Code based cryptography

## Core idea

Code based cryptography is built from error correcting codes.

Normally, error correcting codes help recover messages corrupted by noise. McEliece flips this around: the public key is a disguised code, encryption adds controlled errors, and only someone with the secret decoding structure can remove those errors.

The deck highlights McEliece as a 1978 system that has survived more than 45 years of cryptanalysis. Its main drawback is large public keys, often over 100 KB. 

## McEliece key generation

Bob chooses a code with secret structure that can correct `t` errors.

He has a nice generator matrix `G prime`.

Then he disguises it:

`G = S G prime P`

where:

`S` is a random invertible matrix

`P` is a random permutation matrix

`G` is public

`S`, `G prime`, and `P` are secret

The public key should look like a random code. The private key gives Bob a trapdoor decoder. 

## Encryption

Alice computes:

`c0 = m G`

Then she adds an error vector `e` with exactly `t` bits set:

`c = c0 + e`

The error hides the codeword. To an attacker, this looks like a decoding problem for a random linear code.

## Decryption

Bob uses the permutation secret to move the received word back into the hidden code structure.

Then he uses the secret decoder to remove the errors.

Then he removes the scrambling matrix to recover the original message.

The deck explains this three stage recovery: undo permutation, correct errors using the secret structure, then apply the inverse of the scrambling matrix. 

## Why it is secure

The underlying hard problem is decoding random linear codes. The deck emphasizes that there is no known efficient quantum speedup comparable to Shor’s algorithm, and McEliece has a long cryptanalytic history. But it also warns that not every instance of an NP hard problem is hard, so parameter selection is still critical. 

Senior nuance:

Worst case NP hardness does not automatically mean cryptographic security.

Cryptography depends on average case hardness for the parameter distribution actually used.

## Code based tradeoffs

Strengths:

1. Long conservative history

2. Fast operations

3. Simple conceptual design

4. No known Shor style break

Weaknesses:

1. Huge public keys

2. Less deployment than lattice KEMs

3. More difficult integration into protocols with tight packet limits

4. Not the primary NIST KEM standard in this deck

Senior deployment view:

Code based KEMs are attractive for conservative long term security where public key size is acceptable. They are less attractive for bandwidth constrained handshakes, embedded protocols, and systems with legacy packet size assumptions.

# 2.3 Hash based signatures

## Core idea

Hash based signatures are different from lattice and code systems.

They do not depend on algebraic hardness assumptions like LWE or code decoding. They depend on the security of hash functions.

The deck presents this as the most conservative signature family. Quantum computers do not destroy hash functions the way Shor destroys RSA and ECC. Grover gives a quadratic search speedup, so you use larger hash outputs. 

## One time signatures

The basic building block is a one time signature.

A one time signature key must sign only one message.

This sounds restrictive, but the construction is extremely simple and can be proven secure from hash assumptions.

## Winternitz style intuition

A simplified Winternitz signature uses hash chains.

To sign a value `m`, reveal the `m`th point in a chain.

Verification hashes forward until it reaches the public endpoint.

Problem:

If you reveal a chain point for message `3`, an attacker can hash forward and produce a valid signature for message `4`, `5`, and so on.

The deck shows the fix: sign both `m` and `w minus m`. If the attacker increases one part, they would need to decrease the other, which would require reversing the hash. 

## Message length problem

Signing a long message directly with one huge chain is impossible.

So the message digest is split into chunks. Each chunk is signed separately. More chunks mean larger signatures, but feasible signing.

This is a classic engineering tradeoff:

Smaller base means shorter chains but more chunks.

Larger base means fewer chunks but longer chains.

## The one time problem

The deck is very explicit: never reuse a one time signing key.

If the same key signs two messages, the attacker sees multiple points on the same hash chains and can interpolate forward to forge other values. 

Senior rule:

State corruption is catastrophic in stateful hash signatures.

A VM snapshot, backup restore, crash recovery bug, or multi node signer can accidentally reuse a one time key.

## Merkle trees

To sign many messages, generate many one time key pairs.

Put their public keys as leaves in a Merkle tree.

The root becomes the master public key.

Each signature includes:

1. The one time signature

2. The leaf public key or enough verification data

3. The authentication path up to the Merkle root

The verifier checks that the one time key belongs to the master root.

## XMSS versus SPHINCS plus

The deck compares:

XMSS:

Stateful

Smaller signatures

Must track used keys

State reuse breaks security

SPHINCS plus, standardized as SLH DSA:

Stateless

Larger signatures, often tens of KB

Uses sophisticated tree structures

Safer operationally because no signing state must be preserved

The deck concludes that hash based signatures are the most conservative long term signature choice, but signatures are large and hash based crypto does not give encryption. 

## Tips and tricks for hash signatures

1. Use SLH DSA for ultra conservative roots of trust where signature size is tolerable.

2. Be very careful with XMSS or LMS state management.

3. Never put a stateful hash signer behind a load balancer without strict atomic state allocation.

4. Avoid VM rollback and backup restore hazards.

5. For firmware signing, signature size is often acceptable because updates are already large.

6. For JWTs, cookies, and certificates, large signatures can be painful.

# 3.1 Post quantum TLS

## Why KEMs matter

Traditional ECDH is a shared secret agreement.

Both parties contribute ephemeral secrets and derive the same value.

A KEM is different:

Alice encapsulates to Bob’s public key.

Bob decapsulates with his private key.

Both end with the same secret.

The deck explains that KEMs are a natural fit for many post quantum foundations because lattices and codes do not naturally provide a Diffie Hellman style commutative operation. 

## Hybrid key agreement

Common deployment pattern:

`X25519 plus ML KEM 768`

Why keep X25519?

Because X25519 is mature, fast, small, and very well studied.

Why add ML KEM?

Because X25519 dies under Shor’s algorithm, while ML KEM is designed for post quantum security.

The hybrid result is strong if either component remains secure. The deck calls this a belt and suspenders approach and explicitly mentions hedging against both ML KEM breaks and implementation bugs such as KyberSlash. 

## X Wing

X Wing is an optimized hybrid KEM construction combining classical elliptic curve material with ML KEM material through a careful combiner.

Senior point:

Hybrid does not mean casually concatenate two secrets and hope.

You need domain separation, transcript binding, algorithm identifiers, and a KDF based combiner.

A robust pattern is:

```
input 1: classical shared secret
input 2: post quantum shared secret
input 3: protocol transcript
input 4: algorithm identifiers
output: traffic secret from KDF
```

## TLS deployment reality

The deck describes browser experiments starting in 2016 and later experiments from Google and Cloudflare. It notes that SIKE, an isogeny based candidate, was later completely broken, which is a strong argument for hybrid deployments. 

The biggest real deployment issue was not raw CPU cost. It was size.

Post quantum key shares make the TLS ClientHello larger.

Some middleboxes assumed the ClientHello fit in one packet.

When the ClientHello grew, connections broke.

This is protocol ossification. 

## GREASE

GREASE means sending intentionally unknown protocol values to keep the ecosystem flexible.

The idea is to make broken middleboxes fail early during normal evolution, not years later during urgent migration.

Senior protocol rule:

Extensibility that is not exercised becomes fake extensibility.

## TLS tips and tricks

1. Use hybrid groups where available.

2. Monitor ClientHello sizes.

3. Test through enterprise proxies, mobile carriers, load balancers, and legacy firewalls.

4. Watch HelloRetryRequest behavior.

5. Do not assume internal origin traffic is safe. CDN to origin links matter.

6. Add metrics for selected key exchange group.

7. Treat downgrade resistance as part of the design, not as a configuration option.

# 3.2 Secure messaging

## Signal PQXDH

PQXDH extends Signal’s X3DH initial handshake with a post quantum KEM.

Its goal is to protect initial session establishment against store now decrypt later attacks while preserving the classical security properties of X3DH. The deck emphasizes that this augments classical cryptography rather than replacing it. 

Limitation:

If only the initial handshake is post quantum, then later recovery after compromise may still depend mostly on classical ratcheting.

## Apple PQ3

PQ3 is Apple’s post quantum secure messaging protocol.

The deck describes it as a hybrid protocol combining ECDH and ML KEM in an iMessage style double ratchet. It integrates ML KEM into initialization and ratcheting, not just session setup. 

Key material in the slide:

Classical ECDH keys

Ephemeral session keys

Pre keys for asynchronous start

ML KEM 768 ephemeral keys

ML KEM 1024 pre keys

Root key derivation combines:

Previous root key

ECDH shared secret

KEM shared secret

HKDF

## PQ3 security meaning

PQ3 aims for:

1. Hybrid security

The attacker must break both classical and post quantum components.

2. Forward secrecy

Old message keys are deleted.

3. Post compromise security

Future ratchets can heal after compromise.

4. Store now decrypt later resistance

Recorded traffic should remain protected against future quantum attackers if PQ material was included. 

## PQ3 bandwidth problem

ML KEM public material is much larger than X25519.

The deck compares ML KEM 768 at 1184 bytes and ML KEM 1024 at 1568 bytes against X25519 at 32 bytes. PQ3 therefore cannot send a fresh KEM key with every message in the same way a classical double ratchet can send small DH keys often. It uses a heuristic: refresh about every 50 messages or at least once per week. 

Senior tradeoff:

More frequent PQ ratcheting means faster healing but higher bandwidth.

Less frequent PQ ratcheting means lower bandwidth but slower post quantum healing.

## Signal Triple Ratchet

The deck describes Signal’s Triple Ratchet as a 2025 design with three ratchets:

1. Classical public ratchet based on DH

2. Post quantum public ratchet based on lattice crypto

3. Symmetric ratchet as before

The motivation is that PQXDH protects initial setup, but not full post quantum post compromise security across the conversation. 

## Erasure code innovation

PQ3 may need to resend large KEM material when the peer is offline.

Triple Ratchet instead splits KEM material into chunks using erasure coding. The receiver can reconstruct after receiving enough chunks. This avoids repeatedly sending a full KEM message. 

This is a very good protocol engineering lesson:

When a cryptographic object is too large to send frequently, do not only ask for a smaller algorithm. Also ask whether transport scheduling, coding, acknowledgments, and ratchet design can amortize the cost safely.

## Katana RKEM

The deck introduces Katana as a ratcheting KEM where the ciphertext can also serve as the next public key.

The goal is to save bandwidth by dual purposing values. The slide says previous direct lattice translations were broken and Katana required careful design under a hint MLWE assumption. It reports 37 percent smaller size than a naive Kyber approach at 192 bit security. 

## Messaging implementation traps

1. Offline devices make ratchet synchronization hard.

2. Linked devices multiply state complexity.

3. Acknowledgment handling becomes security critical.

4. Delayed PQ key usage must be modeled explicitly.

5. Multiple ratchets need domain separated root keys.

6. Message loss and reordering must not cause key reuse.

7. Group messaging is much harder than one to one messaging.

# 3.3 Post quantum SSH

## OpenSSH stance

The deck presents OpenSSH as aggressive and early in post quantum key agreement.

Timeline from the slides:

OpenSSH 9.0 in 2022 used `sntrup761x25519 sha512` by default.

OpenSSH 9.9 in 2024 added ML KEM support with `mlkem768x25519 sha256`.

OpenSSH 10.0 made ML KEM default.

OpenSSH 10.1 warns on non post quantum connections.

The deck emphasizes SSH is especially vulnerable to store now decrypt later because attackers can record admin sessions today and decrypt later if the key agreement is broken. 

## Why SSH is easier than TLS

SSH has no global certificate ecosystem like Web PKI.

There are fewer middleboxes.

Protocol negotiation is simpler.

Admin tooling can move faster than browsers and public CAs.

Senior deployment tip:

Upgrade SSH clients and servers early. It is one of the easiest wins in PQ migration.

## SSH tips

1. Prefer hybrid PQ key exchange by default.

2. Keep classical fallback only for compatibility.

3. Log negotiated key exchange algorithms.

4. Alert on non PQ key exchange for sensitive hosts.

5. Prioritize bastion hosts, CI runners, production jump boxes, and network devices.

6. Check embedded SSH servers in appliances because they often lag behind.

# 3.4 Post quantum cryptocurrency

## Why blockchains are special

TLS public keys are usually ephemeral.

Blockchain public keys can become permanently visible on chain.

Bitcoin, Ethereum, and many other systems rely heavily on ECDSA over secp256k1. Shor’s algorithm can recover private keys from public keys. The deck highlights that P2PK scripts store public keys directly, address reuse exposes public keys after first spend, and Taproot exposes tweaked public keys in a way the slide calls a quantum security regression. 

## Three quantum blockchain attacks

The deck defines three attack categories.

1. On spend

The attacker sees a transaction in the mempool, extracts the public key, computes the private key, and tries to steal the funds before confirmation.

This requires a fast quantum architecture.

2. At rest

The attacker targets keys already exposed on chain.

This can be done offline and is easier once cryptographically relevant quantum computers exist.

3. On setup

The attacker breaks fixed protocol parameters, such as trusted setup material, creating a reusable backdoor. 

## Ethereum expanded surface

The deck says Ethereum has more than account ECDSA risk.

It mentions:

Account keys exposed through transactions

Admin keys controlling stablecoins and real world asset systems

No PQC EVM precompiles affecting L2s and bridges

BLS12 381 in proof of stake

KZG trusted setup risk for data availability

The KZG issue is especially important because recovering toxic waste could create a persistent classical backdoor for forging proofs. 

## Dormant assets

The deck raises a policy problem: dormant quantum vulnerable assets, including old BTC that may never migrate.

Possible policies listed include doing nothing, burning, hourglass rate limiting, and digital salvage sidechains. 

Senior point:

Cryptocurrency PQ migration is not only technical. It is governance, economics, game theory, and consensus politics.

## Responsible disclosure with zero knowledge proofs

The deck describes a disclosure idea: prove that a quantum attack circuit meets claimed resource bounds without revealing the circuit.

It also notes an irony: the Groth16 proof system itself relies on pairing friendly elliptic curves, so its soundness is vulnerable to the same broad class of future quantum attacks. 

## Cryptocurrency tips

1. Avoid address reuse.

2. Track exposed public keys, not only balances.

3. Migrate high value wallets before the ecosystem panics.

4. For protocols, design PQ signature verification paths before Q day.

5. For Ethereum style systems, plan precompiles and account abstraction carefully.

6. For bridges and admin keys, rotate to schemes with migration paths.

7. Treat trusted setups as long lived cryptographic liabilities.

# 4 Google threat model

## Why Google is used as a case study

The deck uses Google because it has massive cryptographic diversity, cloud infrastructure, browser influence, and its own quantum computing research. The key idea is risk based prioritization. Not every cryptographic use case has the same urgency. 

## Four prioritization questions

Google’s framework in the slides uses:

1. Attack feasibility

How realistic is the quantum attack?

2. Store now decrypt later risk

Can the attacker record today and decrypt later?

3. Key lifetime

Do keys live for years or decades?

4. Redesign needs

Will migration require deep architecture changes?

The deck’s point is that quantum risk is not uniform. 

## Google use case ranking

The deck ranks encryption in transit as critical because of store now decrypt later.

Firmware signatures are high because keys may be burned into silicon and hard to rotate.

Software signatures are medium because software systems can usually be updated.

PKI and certificates are medium because size becomes a major problem.

Stateless tokens such as JWT are lower in urgency but difficult because size limits are tight. 

## Google algorithm choices

The deck gives a practical mapping:

Encryption in transit:

ML KEM 768 plus X25519 or P 256

Firmware signatures:

SLH DSA because hash based signatures are conservative

Software signatures:

ML DSA plus classical signatures

The important lesson is not “one algorithm everywhere.” It is matching algorithms to use cases. 

## PKI size explosion

Today a certificate chain may be around 3 KB.

With PQ signatures, the slide estimates a three certificate chain can exceed 15 KB.

This affects TLS latency, packet fragmentation, mobile data, and legacy devices that fail on larger packets. 

Senior PKI point:

PQ KEM migration in TLS is easier than PQ signature migration in Web PKI.

KEM material is ephemeral and can be negotiated.

Certificate chains touch CAs, browsers, servers, clients, CT logs, HSMs, OCSP, embedded clients, and middleboxes.

## Threat actors

The deck’s threat actor model:

Nation states likely get quantum capability first and hide it.

Insiders matter for organizations building quantum computers.

Financial attackers arrive later when capability becomes cheaper.

This changes timelines and priorities. 

## Regulatory pressure

The deck mentions United States executive orders, CNSA 2.0, FIPS standards, and European activity from BSI and ANSSI. The conclusion is that government contracts and compliance pressure accelerate migration even before perfect solutions exist. 

## Google lessons

The deck’s final Google lessons are:

1. Prioritize by risk.

2. Match algorithms to use cases.

3. Use hybrid designs.

4. Start experimenting now.

5. Expect PKI and tokens to need creative solutions.

6. Collaborate across industry. 

# 5 How things can go wrong

## Post quantum does not mean unbreakable

The deck gives a reality check: RSA and ECC have decades of cryptanalysis and implementation hardening. Many PQ schemes have only 10 to 20 years of maturity. Security levels are harder to quantify, implementation pitfalls are less understood, and unexpected attacks are possible. 

## 5.1 Unclear security levels

The first problem is the gap between theoretical security and deployed parameters.

A scheme may have a beautiful asymptotic proof, but the real implementation uses finite values such as `n = 512` or `n = 768`.

Asymptotic statement:

Secure as parameters go to infinity.

Concrete question:

Is this exact parameter set secure today against the best known classical and quantum attacks?

The deck warns about average case versus worst case hardness and cites McEliece as an example where “NP hard” language can be misleading if interpreted casually. 

Senior rule:

Never accept “it is based on an NP hard problem” as a complete security argument.

Ask:

1. Which exact problem?

2. Which distribution?

3. Which parameter set?

4. Which known attacks?

5. Which security estimator?

6. Which reduction loss?

7. Which implementation transforms?

## Unknown attack landscape

For RSA, we know the Number Field Sieve and have decades of optimized estimates.

For lattice crypto, new attacks and parameter estimate changes still appear regularly.

During the NIST competition, some candidates with plausible security arguments were broken. The deck uses this to warn that proofs can be correct while assumptions are wrong. 

## 5.2 When quantum computers arrive

The deck challenges a simplistic idea: “encryption is lost but signatures can be fixed.”

Reality depends where the keys live.

Software signatures may be rotated.

Hardware roots of trust may be burned into silicon.

Secure boot keys, debug unlock keys, firmware update keys, and supply chain identity keys can be extremely hard to replace.

## Hardware signature disaster

If hardware signature schemes break, attackers may bypass secure boot, install malicious firmware, unlock debug access, extract secrets, accept counterfeit components, and push fake updates. The deck calls out these consequences directly. 

The cost difference is huge:

Software migration can be weeks or months.

Hardware replacement can be years or decades.

This is why firmware roots and device trust anchors should not be treated as “later.”

## Key agreement is complicated

Simple Diffie Hellman falls hard: if the attacker recovers old private exponents from recorded public keys, past traffic is compromised.

Modern ratcheting protocols are more complex. Signal like protocols use many keys over time, message history, deletion, and ratchet state. Quantum attacks may still be serious, but the retroactive compromise story depends on protocol details. The deck makes this distinction on the key agreement slide. 

# 5.3 Implementation vulnerabilities

## The implementation gap

The deck describes the path:

Perfect algorithm

Real implementation

Bugs, side channels, optimization errors

The algorithm can be secure while the implementation leaks secrets.

This is especially true for PQ crypto because operations are complex and implementations are younger. 

## Goto fail lesson

The Apple TLS goto fail bug is used as a reminder that one duplicated line can bypass signature verification and destroy the security of an otherwise sound protocol. 

Senior lesson:

Cryptographic correctness must be tested structurally, not just with happy path test vectors.

You need negative tests, malformed inputs, fuzzing, formal verification where practical, and code review focused on control flow.

## Side channels

The deck lists timing, power, cache, and electromagnetic leakage.

PQ crypto has many side channel risks because it uses matrix operations, polynomial operations, sampling, compression, decompression, rejection loops, and conditional branches. 

## KyberSlash

KyberSlash is the deck’s real world warning.

It was a timing attack against Kyber implementations caused by variable time division operations in decoding. Timing depended on secret polynomial coefficients. With enough queries, an attacker could recover the secret key. The deck notes that multiple production ready libraries were affected. 

Senior rule:

“Constant time” must be measured and audited. It is not a label.

## Timing in sampling

The deck also discusses Gaussian sampling and rejection sampling as a side channel hazard.

If the number of iterations depends on secret values, timing can reveal information.

For lattice schemes, sampling must be designed and implemented with strict side channel discipline. 

## PQ paradox

The deck’s final paradox is important:

New PQ deployments may initially be less safe in practice than classical crypto because RSA and ECC have decades of hardened implementations, while PQ implementations are still discovering bugs and side channels.

The slide frames maturity as:

Years 1 to 5: vulnerabilities discovered

Years 5 to 10: countermeasures mature

Years 10 and beyond: hardened ecosystem

The final lesson is not to avoid PQ crypto. It is to deploy it carefully, preferably hybrid, with conservative parameters and crypto agility. 

# Final senior engineering checklist

1. Inventory every use of RSA, DH, ECDH, ECDSA, EdDSA, DSA, BLS, and pairing based crypto.

2. Mark which data has confidentiality lifetime greater than 5 years, 10 years, 20 years, and 30 years.

3. Prioritize encryption in transit first because of store now decrypt later.

4. Use hybrid KEMs for transport security.

5. Treat firmware signatures and hardware roots as high priority because they are hard to replace.

6. Do not migrate everything to one PQ algorithm blindly.

7. Use ML KEM for general purpose key establishment.

8. Use ML DSA where efficient PQ signatures are needed.

9. Use SLH DSA where conservative hash based signatures are worth the larger size.

10. Avoid stateful hash signatures unless your state management is formally reliable.

11. Require constant time implementations for KEM decapsulation and signing.

12. Test invalid ciphertexts and malformed signatures.

13. Add telemetry for negotiated algorithms.

14. Design downgrade resistance.

15. Keep classical algorithms during the transition through hybrid composition.

16. Plan for crypto agility so you can replace PQ algorithms if future cryptanalysis changes the landscape.

The final message of the deck is: **post quantum migration is not only an algorithm replacement problem. It is a systems engineering, protocol design, implementation security, operational risk, and ecosystem coordination problem.**



## Big picture

These readings form one coherent topic: quantum computers threaten RSA, finite field Diffie Hellman, and elliptic curve cryptography through Shor’s algorithm, while Grover’s algorithm mainly reduces symmetric security margins. The practical response is post quantum cryptography, especially lattice based key encapsulation and signatures, plus hybrid migration designs that combine classical and post quantum algorithms during the transition. NIST finalized ML KEM, ML DSA, and SLH DSA in August 2024, which is the standards anchor behind much of this reading list. ([NIST][1])

## Required readings

### 1. Jean Philippe Aumasson, Serious Cryptography, Chapter 14, Quantum and Post Quantum

**Core idea:** this chapter is the conceptual bridge from classical cryptography to the post quantum world. It explains why the hardness assumptions behind today’s public key cryptography may fail once large quantum computers exist, and why symmetric cryptography mostly survives with larger parameters.

**Detailed explanation:** the central distinction is between symmetric and asymmetric primitives. AES, SHA 2, SHA 3, HMAC, and modern authenticated encryption are not directly destroyed by quantum computers. Grover’s algorithm gives a quadratic search speedup, so a 128 bit brute force problem behaves more like a 64 bit search against an ideal quantum adversary. The conservative reaction is to use AES 256 and sufficiently long hash outputs when long term quantum resistance matters.

The more serious problem is public key cryptography. RSA depends on integer factorization. Diffie Hellman and elliptic curve Diffie Hellman depend on discrete logarithms. Shor’s algorithm gives an efficient quantum algorithm for both families, so RSA, classic DH, ECDH, DSA, and ECDSA are not acceptable for long term security in a world with cryptographically relevant quantum computers.

The chapter’s main engineering lesson is that post quantum migration is not only about replacing algorithms. Protocols need larger keys, larger ciphertexts, larger signatures, new failure handling, new validation rules, and side channel hardened implementations. This is why KEM based designs, hybrid handshakes, and protocol level agility matter. ([O'Reilly Media][2])

### 2. Gutmann and Neuhaus, Replication of Quantum Factorisation Records with an Eight Bit Home Computer, an Abacus, and a Dog

**Core idea:** this paper is a critique of misleading quantum factoring records. It argues that many publicized quantum factorization demonstrations do not show scalable Shor style cryptanalytic progress, because they exploit prior knowledge, compilation tricks, or problem simplifications.

**Detailed explanation:** the paper’s provocative claim is that some reported quantum factorization milestones can be replicated by absurdly weak classical systems, including a VIC 20, an abacus, and even a dog, because the demonstrations are not factoring in the cryptographically meaningful sense. They often encode the answer into the construction, reduce the instance using special algebraic properties, or solve a toy instance that has no relevance to RSA scale or ECC scale cryptanalysis. ([IACR Eprint Archive][3])

The cryptographic lesson is very important: “largest number factored on a quantum device” is a bad security metric. What matters is whether a fault tolerant quantum computer can run a scalable implementation of Shor’s algorithm with enough logical qubits, gates, error correction, and runtime to attack RSA or elliptic curves. The paper is therefore not saying quantum cryptanalysis is impossible. It is saying that toy factoring demos are poor evidence for cryptographic danger. ([Schneier on Security][4])

### 3. Google Quantum AI, Safeguarding Cryptocurrency by Disclosing Quantum Vulnerabilities Responsibly

**Core idea:** Google argues that quantum vulnerabilities in cryptocurrency should be disclosed like serious security vulnerabilities: enough detail to motivate migration, but not enough to hand attackers an optimized weapon.

**Correction:** I found the public Google Research Blog item dated March 31, 2026, not 2025. I am treating it as the intended reading. ([Google Research][5])

**Detailed explanation:** the blog is attached to a Google Quantum AI whitepaper on elliptic curve cryptocurrency risk. The central target is secp256k1 and related elliptic curve systems used in Bitcoin, Ethereum, and many other blockchain systems. If a public key is visible before a transaction finalizes, a sufficiently powerful quantum computer could derive the private key quickly enough to race or steal the transaction. This is different from the usual “old encrypted data can be decrypted later” story. In cryptocurrencies, the danger can become an active “on spend” attack against public mempool transactions. ([arXiv][6])

The whitepaper claims improved resource estimates for solving the 256 bit elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem, including circuits with fewer than 1200 logical qubits and fewer than 90 million Toffoli gates, or fewer than 1450 logical qubits and fewer than 70 million Toffoli gates. It also states that on certain superconducting assumptions, such attacks could run in minutes with fewer than half a million physical qubits. These are not current machines, but they materially affect migration urgency. ([arXiv][6])

The disclosure lesson is subtle: publish enough to let communities verify urgency and plan migration, but withhold details that would accelerate real attacks. That is why the paper uses a zero knowledge proof approach to validate parts of the resource estimate without fully disclosing the attack construction. ([arXiv][6])

## Optional readings

### 4. The Joy of Cryptography, Chapter 20, Post Quantum Cryptography

**Core idea:** this is likely the most proof oriented introduction in the list. It frames post quantum cryptography through formal security definitions and reductions rather than only engineering migration.

**Detailed explanation:** the public online table of contents confirms Chapter 20 is Post Quantum Cryptography, but the site says most chapters beyond the first few become available online in July 2026. So I cannot verify the full chapter text from the public source. Based on the book’s stated focus, expect the chapter to explain how to reason about post quantum schemes using the same provable security mindset used for encryption, signatures, key exchange, and zero knowledge. ([joyofcryptography.com][7])

The value of this chapter is conceptual discipline. Many engineers think “quantum safe” means “replace ECDH with ML KEM.” A provable security view asks sharper questions: what is the adversary model, what is the security game, what hardness assumption is used, how tight is the reduction, and what changes when the adversary has quantum computation?

### 5. Patton and Schwabe, Prepping for Post Quantum, Beginner’s Guide to Lattice Cryptography

**Core idea:** this is a practical lattice primer. It explains the intuition behind lattices, learning with errors, noisy linear equations, and why these problems are useful for encryption, key encapsulation, and signatures.

**Detailed explanation:** lattice cryptography hides secrets inside structured linear algebra with controlled noise. The rough idea is that solving exact linear equations is easy, but solving large modular linear equations with hidden errors is believed to be hard, even for quantum adversaries. Modern schemes such as ML KEM and ML DSA use module lattice structure to make the schemes efficient while preserving a strong hardness story. ([The Cloudflare Blog][8])

This reading is especially useful because it connects the math to implementation questions: why public keys and ciphertexts are larger than elliptic curve values, why noise must be sampled carefully, why decryption failure probabilities matter, and why constant time arithmetic is non negotiable.

### 6. Lyubashevsky, Basic Lattice Cryptography

**Core idea:** this is a deeper technical explanation of the concepts behind Kyber, now ML KEM, and Dilithium, now ML DSA.

**Detailed explanation:** Lyubashevsky’s tutorial focuses on the mathematical design decisions behind the two main lattice schemes standardized by NIST. It explains module learning with errors, module short integer solution style assumptions, polynomial rings, number theoretic transforms, rejection sampling, and the transform from underlying hard problems to practical KEMs and signatures. ([IACR Eprint Archive][9])

The core takeaway is that ML KEM and ML DSA are not arbitrary new algorithms. They are carefully engineered lattice constructions that trade some conservative simplicity for performance, compactness, and implementability. This is why they became primary standards.

### 7. Barbosa et al., X Wing, The Hybrid KEM You Have Been Looking For

**Core idea:** X Wing is a hybrid KEM that combines X25519 with ML KEM 768 to get security from both classical elliptic curve Diffie Hellman and post quantum lattice assumptions.

**Detailed explanation:** the purpose of a hybrid KEM is migration safety. If ML KEM were later weakened, X25519 still protects against classical adversaries. If a future quantum computer breaks X25519, ML KEM still protects against quantum adversaries. X Wing is designed as a concrete, efficient construction rather than a generic combiner bolted onto two independent KEMs. ([IACR Eprint Archive][10])

This is a deployment oriented paper. Its importance is not only the proof but the engineering shape: a single construction suitable for protocols that need a sensible default during the migration period.

### 8. Apple SEAR, iMessage with PQ3

**Core idea:** PQ3 is Apple’s large scale deployment of post quantum secure messaging, using hybrid initialization and ongoing post quantum rekeying inside conversations.

**Detailed explanation:** many systems add post quantum protection only at session start. Apple’s argument is stronger: long lived messaging conversations need continuous healing. PQ3 periodically injects new post quantum key material into the conversation so that future message keys can become safe again after a compromise. Apple describes this as post quantum rekeying and self healing after key compromise. ([Apple Security Research][11])

The protocol lesson is that secure messaging is harder than TLS style handshakes. Messaging needs asynchronous delivery, multiple devices, conversation state, forward secrecy, post compromise security, and metadata resistant operational behavior. PQ3 is important because it moves post quantum cryptography from theory into consumer scale deployment.

### 9. Linker, Sasse, and Basin, Formal Analysis of Apple’s iMessage PQ3 Protocol

**Core idea:** this paper formally models PQ3 and proves its intended security properties using symbolic verification.

**Detailed explanation:** Apple’s blog says PQ3 is secure, but a formal analysis asks whether the protocol actually satisfies precise properties under a defined adversary model. The paper presents formal verification of PQ3 as a device to device messaging protocol with security against quantum capable adversaries. It models Apple identity services, the post quantum initialization phase, and the ratchet design. ([USENIX][12])

The important lesson is that post quantum deployment must be verified at the protocol level. A secure KEM does not automatically imply a secure messaging protocol. State transitions, replay behavior, device identity, key updates, and compromise recovery all need formal treatment.

### 10. Bhargavan et al., Formal Verification of PQXDH

**Core idea:** this paper analyzes Signal’s PQXDH, the post quantum extension of X3DH for asynchronous secure messaging.

**Detailed explanation:** X3DH is the initial key agreement mechanism used to start Signal style conversations. PQXDH adds post quantum protection so that initial shared secrets remain confidential against “harvest now decrypt later” attacks. The paper formally specifies PQXDH and analyzes it with ProVerif and CryptoVerif. ([USENIX][13])

The key distinction from PQ3 is scope. PQXDH focuses on the initial asynchronous key agreement. PQ3 also emphasizes continuous post quantum rekeying inside the conversation. Both are necessary topics: one protects session start, the other protects long running conversation evolution.

### 11. Dodis et al., Triple Ratchet

**Core idea:** Triple Ratchet proposes a more bandwidth efficient hybrid secure Signal style protocol.

**Detailed explanation:** post quantum ratcheting is expensive because lattice public keys and ciphertexts are much larger than elliptic curve values. A naive post quantum ratchet can send large KEM material too frequently. Triple Ratchet tries to preserve hybrid security while reducing repeated transmission of post quantum material. ([IACR Eprint Archive][14])

The core contribution is efficiency under realistic messaging conditions. It treats bandwidth not as a secondary optimization but as a security deployment constraint. If a post quantum messenger is too heavy, platforms will delay deployment or silently weaken guarantees.

### 12. Westerbaan, The State of the Post Quantum Internet

**Core idea:** this reading surveys deployment reality. It explains what has actually changed on the Internet and what remains difficult.

**Detailed explanation:** Cloudflare’s 2024 update notes that the field changed quickly: Kyber became ML KEM, signature candidates evolved, and SIKE suffered a catastrophic attack. It also reports early deployment data, including that almost 2 percent of TLS 1.3 connections established with Cloudflare were protected with post quantum mechanisms at that point. ([The Cloudflare Blog][15])

This reading is valuable because it prevents a purely academic view. Real Internet deployment has constraints: packet size, handshake fragmentation, middlebox behavior, certificate chains, CPU cost, client compatibility, monitoring, and failure rollback.

### 13. Schmieg, Kölbl, and Endignoux, Google’s Threat Model for Post Quantum Cryptography

**Core idea:** Google’s threat model prioritizes migration based on actual risk, especially confidentiality loss from stored traffic and authenticity failure from broken signatures.

**Detailed explanation:** Google’s later hybrid deployment blog explicitly says its migration approach is driven by its post quantum threat model. The key idea is that confidentiality is already at risk for long lived secrets because adversaries can record encrypted traffic now and decrypt it later. Signatures are different: they usually do not expose old confidentiality, but broken signature schemes can create catastrophic authenticity failures once a quantum attacker exists. ([bughunters.google.com][16])

The practical takeaway is prioritization. First protect high value encrypted traffic using hybrid KEMs. Then migrate signatures before quantum computers arrive, because authentication roots, firmware signatures, certificates, package signing, and blockchains can have very long lifetimes.

### 14. Bernstein et al., KyberSlash

**Core idea:** KyberSlash shows that even standardized post quantum algorithms can be broken in practice by timing leakage if implementations are not constant time.

**Detailed explanation:** the attacks exploit secret dependent division timings in Kyber implementations, including reference style implementations. The authors demonstrate key recovery on platforms such as Raspberry Pi 2 and Arm Cortex M4, with recovery times ranging from minutes to hours depending on the variant. ([KyberSlash][17])

The lesson is severe: post quantum security is not only a math problem. Lattice schemes use modular arithmetic, compression, decompression, rejection sampling, and polynomial operations. If any operation branches, divides, indexes memory, or runs for variable time based on secret data, the implementation can leak the private key.

### 15. Ravi et al., Lattice Based Key Sharing Schemes, A Survey

**Core idea:** this survey maps the evolution of lattice based public key encryption, KEMs, and key exchange schemes.

**Detailed explanation:** it covers the design space of lattice based key sharing schemes, including theoretical assumptions, NIST candidates, algorithmic structure, and software and hardware performance considerations. ([IACR Eprint Archive][18])

Its value is comparative. Instead of learning only ML KEM, you see the family tree: LWE, Ring LWE, Module LWE, NTRU style constructions, reconciliation based key exchange, and KEM transforms. This helps you understand why some designs were standardized and others were not.

### 16. Mosca and Piani, Quantum Threat Timeline Report

**Core idea:** this is a risk management report about when cryptographically relevant quantum computers may arrive and how organizations should plan migration.

**Detailed explanation:** the 2024 report collected views from 32 global experts on quantum computing progress and cyber risk. Its role is not to predict an exact date. Its role is to convert uncertainty into planning pressure. ([Global Risk Institute][19])

The essential framework is: migration must finish before the threat arrives, and data confidentiality may need protection before that because of stored traffic. So the relevant question is not “do quantum computers exist today?” The relevant question is “will my data or trust anchor still matter when they do?”

### 17. Alwen, Hale, Mularczyk, and Tian, Flexible Hybrid PQ MLS Combiner

**Core idea:** this draft addresses post quantum migration for MLS, the group messaging protocol behind modern secure group communication.

**Detailed explanation:** MLS groups are more complex than two party messaging because group membership, commits, epochs, and sender state all evolve. The combiner design uses a traditional MLS session and a post quantum MLS session, then combines exported secrets to seed post quantum guarantees into the traditional session. This amortizes the cost of post quantum operations. ([Messaging Layer Security (MLS)][20])

The core deployment idea is amortization. You do not necessarily need to pay the full post quantum cost on every group operation. You can inject post quantum entropy periodically or strategically while keeping the main group protocol efficient.

### 18. Randriambololona, The Syzygy Distinguisher

**Core idea:** this is a cryptanalytic result against structured code based cryptography, especially alternant and Goppa codes.

**Detailed explanation:** the paper presents a new distinguisher for alternant and Goppa codes with subexponential complexity in the error correcting capability. It is notable because it applies to codes used in Classic McEliece, a major code based post quantum candidate. ([ACM Digital Library][21])

A distinguisher is not automatically a full key recovery attack. But it matters because code based cryptography often relies on the assumption that the public code hides exploitable structure. If a distinguisher detects structure more efficiently than expected, designers must reassess margins, parameters, and proof assumptions.

### 19. Miller, OpenSSH, Post Quantum Cryptography

**Core idea:** OpenSSH is already deploying post quantum key agreement by default and warning users when connections do not use it.

**Detailed explanation:** OpenSSH says it has offered post quantum key agreement by default since version 9.0 in April 2022 using sntrup761x25519 sha512. It later added mlkem768x25519 sha256 in OpenSSH 9.9 and made that the default in OpenSSH 10.0 in April 2025. OpenSSH 10.1 warns when a connection uses non post quantum key agreement. ([OpenSSH][22])

This is one of the clearest real world migration examples. It uses hybrid key agreement, so even if the post quantum component is later weakened, the classical X25519 component still preserves the previous classical security level. ([OpenSSH][22])

### 20. Craig Gidney, Why Have Quantum Computers Not Factored 21 Yet?

**Core idea:** factoring 21 is a misleading benchmark for quantum progress.

**Detailed explanation:** Gidney explains that factoring 15 had special shortcuts, while factoring 21 requires a much less forgiving circuit structure. The fact that machines have not cleanly factored 21 using a meaningful scalable approach does not imply there has been no quantum progress. It shows that toy factoring numbers are bad benchmarks. ([algassert.com][23])

This reading pairs well with Gutmann and Neuhaus. Both warn against benchmark theater. For cryptography, the important metrics are logical qubits, Toffoli count, circuit depth, error correction overhead, physical error rate, and wall clock runtime.

### 21. Babbush et al., Securing Elliptic Curve Cryptocurrencies Against Quantum Vulnerabilities

**Core idea:** this is the technical whitepaper behind Google’s cryptocurrency disclosure. It gives resource estimates and mitigation strategy for quantum attacks on elliptic curve based cryptocurrencies.

**Detailed explanation:** the paper estimates that Shor’s algorithm for 256 bit elliptic curve discrete logarithms can be implemented with fewer logical qubits and gates than previously appreciated. It focuses on secp256k1, the curve used by Bitcoin and many other systems. It distinguishes fast clock architectures such as superconducting and photonic machines from slow clock architectures such as neutral atom and ion trap machines, because attack feasibility depends heavily on runtime. ([arXiv][6])

The cryptocurrency specific insight is that vulnerability is not limited to abandoned coins. Active spending can reveal a public key before finality, creating a transaction race if a quantum attacker can derive the private key fast enough. The paper also discusses harder governance questions: dormant assets, smart contracts, proof of stake systems, data availability sampling, and whether communities need policy for quantum exposed abandoned funds. ([arXiv][6])

## The core idea of the whole reading set

The central message is this:

Classical public key cryptography is living on borrowed time, not because quantum computers can break it today, but because migration is slow, data lifetimes are long, and the engineering surface is huge. The winning strategy is not panic. It is staged migration: inventory cryptography, protect confidentiality first with hybrid KEMs, migrate signatures before quantum capable attackers arrive, verify protocols formally, harden implementations against side channels, and treat deployment constraints as first class cryptographic requirements.
